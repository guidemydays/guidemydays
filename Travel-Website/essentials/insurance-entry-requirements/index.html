Insurance Entry Requirements

Required to enter, not just recommended.

Insurance is sometimes a legal entry, visa, or residence-permit condition — not merely protection. Schengen visa applicants, foreign visitors to Cuba and the Galápagos, and Turkish residence-permit applicants need proof; the United States, Switzerland and Japan impose no general tourist requirement. See Travel Insurance for coverage types and buying guidance.

Legally Required — Entry, Visa or Residence Permit

In these four cases, proof of insurance is a formal condition rather than general advice. The trigger differs by destination: nationality and visa status, a trip to a specific territory, or a residence-permit application.

Schengen Area29 countries
Visa applicants
Anyone applying for a Schengen visa must show travel medical insurance before the visa is issued — the requirement sits inside the visa application, not a border check. Visa-exempt nationalities (the US, UK, Canada and most others on short trips) are not checked for it on arrival, but the same coverage protects against the same costs either way.

CubaForeign visitors
Foreign visitors
Cuba requires foreign visitors to have non-Cuban medical insurance. Coverage is often included in airline tickets on flights originating in the United States; confirm that with the airline before departure. Travelers without accepted coverage can buy an official local policy after arrival. Ordinary US health insurance, Medicare and Medicaid are not accepted for care in Cuba.

Galápagos entry
Mainland Ecuador has no general tourist-insurance requirement. The Galápagos special regime is different: foreign tourists must have private health insurance before entering the province. Confirm acceptable proof with the airline or tour operator before the domestic flight from Quito or Guayaquil.

Residence permit
Tourists do not need to show a special insurance policy at the border for a short stay. Residence-permit applicants are a different case: they must document qualifying coverage for the full period requested, unless an official exemption or an accepted public or reciprocal-health arrangement applies.

Not Required — But the Bill Can Wreck the Trip

Nothing below is a general tourist-entry requirement. All three are places where a hospital visit can cost more than the trip and a home-country health plan may provide limited or no overseas coverage.

United StatesNo entry requirement
The US has no general insurance requirement for tourists. Medical care can be exceptionally expensive for someone paying without insurance, and a home-country plan may exclude or sharply limit US treatment. Confirm both emergency-care and medical-evacuation coverage before departure.No general tourist-entry requirement
SwitzerlandSchengen visa rule only
Switzerland carries no general visitor-entry insurance mandate beyond the Schengen visa-applicant rule (€30,000 minimum — see above). Healthcare is expensive, so visa-exempt travelers should check that their policy's medical and evacuation limits are adequate rather than treating the Schengen minimum as a target.No requirement beyond the Schengen visa rule
JapanNo tourist-entry requirement
Japan does not currently require tourists to show private medical insurance at entry; its foreign ministry strongly recommends buying it. Immigration can scrutinize a returning visitor with reported unpaid medical bills, but a broader pre-entry insurance mandate remains a policy proposal rather than a current entry rule. Check your own plan carefully — US Medicare and Medicaid do not cover care in Japan.Recommended, not required for entry
