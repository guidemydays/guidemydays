Medical Emergency Abroad

The first ten minutes decide the rest, and they follow a fixed order.

What to do, in order, for a medical emergency on a trip: call local emergency services, involve the insurer once help is moving, understand evacuation approval, carry usable medical information, and keep the records a claim may require. This is the response sequence itself — not a hospital phrasebook or a medical-kit packing list.

Call the destination's local emergency number first — not your insurer. Getting help moving is the priority; the assistance line comes next. Save the correct number before travel and confirm it in the destination's official travel advice. The number 112 is guaranteed across the EU and works in some other countries and GSM networks, but it is not a universal substitute for the local number.
1. Call for Help, in This Order
1
The local emergency number — first
A local dispatcher can send the appropriate emergency service and direct you to a suitable facility; an insurer's call center cannot replace that response. Give your location, callback number, and the person's condition as clearly as you can.
2
Your travel insurer's 24-hour assistance line — once help is already moving
The number is on the insurance card or policy documents — save it offline and on paper before you leave. The assistance team can explain covered facilities, payment arrangements, and any authorization needed for evacuation or expensive treatment; direct billing is policy- and hospital-dependent.
3
Traveling with someone? Split the two calls
One phone dials the emergency number, the other dials the insurer, at the same time. Neither call should be sitting on hold behind the other.
2. How Emergency Evacuation Actually Activates
1
Do not arrange a costly evacuation without authorization
Medical evacuation usually means transport to the nearest facility that can provide suitable treatment. Repatriation home may be a separate benefit. Except when immediate life-saving action leaves no choice, let the insurer's assistance team and treating clinicians coordinate transport so you do not jeopardize coverage.
2
Open the case early
Tell the assistance team what the local clinicians have said and whether the facility can provide the required care. Medical review, authorization, and transport can take time, so contact the insurer as soon as the immediate emergency response is underway.
3
Eligibility is medical necessity, not preference
Coverage commonly depends on the policy's medical-necessity test and approval process — not simply on a preference to be treated at home. Read the definitions for evacuation, repatriation, pre-existing conditions, and excluded activities before travel.
4
What it costs without coverage
The U.S. State Department says an air ambulance back to the United States can cost $20,000–$200,000, depending on location and condition. Destination, distance, aircraft, clinical staff, and policy wording all matter, so compare evacuation limits and terms rather than premium alone.
3. What to Carry — Prepared Before Anything Happens
Insurance card + assistance line number
Save it offline, put a paper copy in your bag, and share it with a travel companion or trusted contact.
Prescriptions, by generic name
Brand names differ by country — a foreign pharmacist may not recognize yours, but the generic name travels everywhere.
Allergies, conditions, and medication doses
A concise medical summary a treating team can read without relying on you being able to speak.
Photo of your passport data page
Speeds up hospital intake and any embassy contact that follows.
Your embassy or consulate's local number
For the specific country you're in — not a general head-office line back home.
4. Inside a Foreign ER
1
Be prepared for a deposit or upfront payment
Payment rules vary. In many locations a hospital may require payment or a deposit before services, even when you are insured; elsewhere reciprocal or public coverage may apply. Do not delay emergency help to settle billing. Contact the insurer once care is underway and request itemized bills, medical reports, prescriptions, and proof of payment.
2
Ask for qualified language help
Ask the hospital whether it has an interpreter service and ask the insurer what language support it provides. For important consent or treatment decisions, use a qualified interpreter where available rather than relying only on machine translation or a child or bystander.
3
Do not choose public versus private by rule of thumb
For an emergency, follow the local dispatcher and treating team's direction. Once the person is stable, the insurer can advise whether another facility is clinically appropriate and covered. The nearest private hospital is not automatically the best emergency destination.
5. Documenting and Filing the Claim
1
What to gather
The itemized bill, proof of payment, and the treating physician's report. If you paid in a foreign currency, keep the original receipt rather than just noting a converted total — insurers ask for it as filed.
2
Check your filing deadline — don't assume it
Notify the insurer and file within the exact deadlines in your policy; do not rely on a generic 30- or 90-day rule. Ask whether notification is required while you are still abroad and keep confirmation of every submission.
3
Translation, if the paperwork isn't in English
Ask the insurer whether it needs a translation and, if so, what kind it accepts before paying for one. Keep the original-language documents and any translation together.
4
Track the case
Keep the claim number, submission receipt, and a dated record of calls and documents. Processing times vary; respond promptly to requests and use the insurer's complaint or appeal route if a decision does not match the policy wording.
