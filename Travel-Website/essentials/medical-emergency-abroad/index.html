Medical Emergency Abroad

The first ten minutes decide the rest, and they follow a fixed order.

What to do, in order, for a genuine medical emergency on a trip: who to call first, how your insurer's evacuation coverage actually activates, what to carry before anything happens, what a foreign ER visit looks like, and how to file the claim once you're home. This is the response sequence itself — not a phrasebook for the hospital and not a packing list for a medical kit, which are their own separate topics.

Call the local emergency number first — not your insurer. Getting help moving is the priority; your travel insurer's job starts once that's already happening. If you don't know your destination's number, see "Emergency numbers, region by region" below, ask any local (hotel desk, restaurant staff, a passerby), or dial 112 — it's pre-programmed as a fallback on most mobile networks worldwide and will often connect even where it isn't the official number.
1. Call for Help, in This Order
1
The local emergency number — always first
A local dispatcher gets an ambulance moving in minutes and knows the nearest capable facility; an insurer's call center, however good, does neither. This step is the same regardless of what coverage you're carrying.
2
Your travel insurer's 24-hour assistance line — once help is already moving
The number is on the back of your insurance card or in the policy PDF — save it offline before you leave (see the checklist below). This is the call that unlocks direct billing at many hospitals and starts an evacuation review if one turns out to be needed.
3
Traveling with someone? Split the two calls
One phone dials the emergency number, the other dials the insurer, at the same time. Neither call should be sitting on hold behind the other.
2. How Emergency Evacuation Actually Activates
1
You don't book it — the assistance line does
Evacuation is medically necessary transport to a facility that can actually treat you, or home once you're stable. It's coordinated directly between your insurer's assistance line and the treating doctor, not arranged by the traveler.
2
Flag it early, even before it's confirmed
Say plainly on that first call that evacuation may be needed. The medical review and transport booking that follow can take hours even once approved, so the case being flagged early is what keeps that clock from starting later than it has to.
3
Eligibility is medical necessity, not preference
A policy covers evacuation when the treating doctor and the insurer's own medical team agree the local facility can't adequately treat the condition — not simply because a traveler would rather be treated at home.
4
What it costs without coverage
An uninsured medical evacuation commonly runs into six figures once it's more than a short regional transfer — a large part of why the coverage limit on a policy matters more than the premium.
3. What to Carry — Prepared Before Anything Happens
Insurance card + assistance line number
A screenshot saved to your phone, not just an email you'd need signal to open.
Prescriptions, by generic name
Brand names differ by country — a foreign pharmacist may not recognize yours, but the generic name travels everywhere.
Allergies and blood type, in writing
Something a treating team can read in seconds, without relying on you being able to speak.
Photo of your passport data page
Speeds up hospital intake and any embassy contact that follows.
Your embassy or consulate's local number
For the specific country you're in — not a general head-office line back home.
4. Inside a Foreign ER
1
Expect to pay upfront
Outside a handful of countries with reciprocal or national coverage arrangements, a hospital will typically ask for payment or a card before treatment — this is standard practice there, not a sign anything is wrong. Ask for an itemized bill from the start; the claim afterward depends on it.
2
Language doesn't have to be a barrier
Ask whether an English-speaking doctor is available — common at hospitals in tourist-heavy areas. Where one isn't, your insurer's assistance line can often conference in a translator, and many hospitals separately use phone-based interpreter services.
3
A private hospital is usually the better first stop for anything serious
Where one exists nearby, insurers often specify it directly — worth asking the assistance line rather than assuming the closest facility is the right one.
5. Filing the Claim, Once You're Home
1
What to gather
The itemized bill, proof of payment, and the treating physician's report. If you paid in a foreign currency, keep the original receipt rather than just noting a converted total — insurers ask for it as filed.
2
Check your filing deadline — don't assume it
Most policies set a claim window from the date of treatment, commonly around 90 days. It varies by provider, and it's the kind of detail that's easy to miss while you're still traveling.
3
Translation, if the paperwork isn't in English
A medical report in another language usually needs a certified translation before an English-speaking insurer will process the claim — worth starting early rather than at the deadline.
4
Set the right timing expectation
Reimbursement on an international claim typically takes weeks, not days. Knowing that going in avoids a second round of stress on top of the trip itself.
