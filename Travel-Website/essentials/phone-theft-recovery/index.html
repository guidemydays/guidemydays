Phone Theft & Tech Recovery Abroad

The phone is gone, and your accounts are the real emergency.

What to do, in order, once a phone is stolen on a trip: locking it remotely before anything else, filing the police report insurance will require, what to act on first given what was actually exposed, getting back online to reach the people who need to hear from you, and filing the claim once you're home. This is the response sequence itself — not scam prevention, which lives on Scams & Tourist Traps, and not which card to carry, which lives on Cards & ATM.

Lock the phone remotely before you do anything else — from any other device, in minutes. Everything below matters, but a phone that is still unlocked is a live risk to your accounts for every minute it stays that way. If you don't have another device to hand, borrow one — a hotel desk, a travel companion, or any browser will do for the first step.
1. Lock It Down Remotely, First
1
iPhone — Lost Mode, from any browser or device
At icloud.com/find (or the Find My app on another Apple device), select the missing iPhone and choose Mark As Lost. This locks the screen with a passcode, displays a phone number and message you choose, and suspends Apple Pay on the device — without needing the phone's own passcode. It does not erase anything, so it doesn't undo an insurance claim or a police report that references the device's condition.
2
Android — Find My Device, the same way
At android.com/find, signed into the same Google account, select the device and choose Secure Device. It locks the screen, can show a message and callback number on the lock screen, and signs the device out of the Google account for anything that requires re-authentication.
3
Suspend the line with your carrier
A separate step from locking the device — call or use your carrier's app to suspend the SIM or eSIM. This stops calls, texts and data on that number immediately and prevents roaming charges from racking up on a phone you no longer control.
4
Erase remotely — the fallback, not the first move
Erase (the same Find My / Find My Device screen) wipes the device the next time it connects to the internet. Use it only once you accept you won't get the phone back, since it also erases the ability to track its location going forward. Lost Mode or Secure Device already protects your accounts without giving that up — erase later if you need to.
2. File the Police Report
1
In person, the same day — this is mandatory for insurance
A travel insurance or device claim needs an official report number, not a description of what happened. This has to be filed in person at a police station — a commissariat in France, a comisaría in Spain and most of Latin America, a questura or polizia station in Italy — while you're still in the country. It cannot usually be done after you've flown home.
2
Find the right station
Ask your hotel desk for the nearest station that handles reports for tourists — in cities with heavy tourist traffic there is often one used to the process and quicker than a random local precinct.
3
Bring, and know
Your passport, the approximate time and location of the theft, and the phone's IMEI number if you have it saved (dial *#06# on the phone before you travel to find it, or check the original box or your Apple/Google account online — Find My and Find My Device both display it for a registered device).
4
Say theft, not loss — the wording matters for the claim
Describe it as stolen (robado / volé / rubato / gestohlen), not lost — insurers treat the two very differently and most travel policies exclude simple loss entirely. Ask for a copy of the report itself (a denuncia or déclaration) with its reference number before you leave the station; the claim can't be filed without it.
3. What Was Exposed — Act on This First
1
Banking and payment apps
Call your bank's international line (usually on the back of the card, or saved separately from your phone — see the checklist below) and ask them to block any card tied to Apple Pay or Google Pay on that device. A locked phone can sometimes still authorize a payment that was already queued before it locked.
2
Email — change this password first
From another device, change your primary email password before anything else. Email is the recovery path for almost every other account, so securing it first is what stops one stolen phone from becoming several compromised accounts.
3
Two-factor authenticator apps
If an authenticator app lived only on that phone, use the backup codes you saved when you first set up two-factor on each account, or that service's own account-recovery flow. Keep a running note of which accounts you still can't get back into — you'll need it for both the recovery process and, if any of them are financial, your bank.
4
Saved passwords
If a password manager was unlocked, or the phone itself was unlocked, when it was taken, treat every password stored on it as compromised. Work outward from what matters most — email and banking, then anything with a stored payment method — rather than trying to change everything at once.
4. Getting Back Online
1
A temporary SIM or eSIM on a backup device
Any carrier shop, kiosk or supermarket counter in most countries sells a prepaid SIM with your passport — this gets a local number back on a spare phone or a device a travel companion can lend you. See Phone & SIM Abroad for how to buy one and what it costs by region.
2
Restoring WhatsApp or Signal
Reinstall the app on the new device with the new temporary number, or — if you kept your original number through an eSIM transfer — restore your chat history from your last cloud backup. Either way, contacts see a "new device" notice, which is expected and not itself a security problem.
3
A hotel business center or a borrowed device — enough for now
Fine for reading one-time codes and checking email while you arrange a permanent fix. Avoid logging into banking or anything with saved payment details from a shared or public computer — use it for recovery steps only, not day-to-day access.
4
Tell close contacts through a different channel
A quick email or a call from the hotel to whoever you'd normally text lets them know your number is temporarily down — so a message that looks like it's from your old number, asking for money or codes, is recognized as suspicious rather than acted on.
5. Filing the Insurance Claim
1
What to gather
The police report and its reference number, proof you owned the device (a receipt, or your Apple/Google account showing it registered to you), and the IMEI if you have it. Keep the original police report — insurers generally want it, not a photo of a photo.
2
Check which policy is actually covering this
Travel insurance typically covers a stolen phone under "personal effects," usually with a per-item sub-limit well below the phone's value. A dedicated phone or gadget insurance policy is a separate product and usually pays out closer to replacement cost — check both if you're not sure which one applies. See Travel Insurance for how personal-effects coverage generally works.
3
Notify quickly, then file within the window
Most insurers want the theft reported to them within 24–48 hours, with a full written claim inside a separate, longer deadline — commonly around 30 days. Both windows vary by insurer, so confirm yours rather than assuming.
4
What insurers typically ask for
The police report, proof of ownership, and often a short written statement of what happened, in your own words. Some also ask whether the device was ever left unattended — answer plainly; it affects the outcome less often than travelers assume, but a claim written to avoid the question reads worse than one that just answers it.
