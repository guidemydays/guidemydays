1.Tipu Sultan's Summer Palace
Hyder Ali began this teak palace inside Bangalore Fort in 1781, and his son Tipu Sultan finished it a decade later. The two-storey structure is carved wood from top to bottom — pillars, arches and balconies. The ground floor holds a small ASI museum on Tipu's reign, including a replica of Tipu's Tiger, with a nominal ticket bought at the gate.

2.Bangalore Fort
Kempe Gowda I raised a mud fort here in 1537 when he founded the city; Hyder Ali rebuilt it in stone in 1761, and his son Tipu Sultan strengthened it further. British troops under Lord Cornwallis breached the walls near this spot in the 1791 Siege of Bangalore, and most of the fort was demolished over the following century. The Delhi Gate, two bastions and a small Ganapathi temple are what remain.

3.Dodda Basavana Gudi
Also called the Bull Temple, Kempe Gowda I raised this Vijayanagara-style shrine in 1537 around a single Nandi cut from one block of granite — 4.5m tall and 6m long, among the largest monolithic Nandi statues in the world. The bull sits garlanded under a low stone canopy in Basavanagudi. Every year the Kadalekai Parishe groundnut fair honours the deity in the surrounding lanes.

1.Vidhana Soudha
Karnataka's legislature sits behind this granite Neo-Dravidian facade, built in the 1950s at Chief Minister Kengal Hanumanthaiah's insistence on a "people's palace" rather than a colonial bungalow. 2.National Gallery of Modern Art
A hundred-year-old mansion on Palace Road — built for the merchant Haji Sir Ismail Sait and later renamed for owner Manickavelu Mudaliar — now holds roughly 500 works spanning the 18th century to the present. Raja Ravi Varma and Amrita Sher-Gil anchor the collection, alongside Jamini Roy and Bengal School painters, spread through a corridor, small rooms and two full halls.

3.Government Museum & Venkatappa Art Gallery
Founded in 1865, the second-oldest museum in India sits in Chief Engineer Richard Sankey's red Neoclassical hall on Kasturba Road. Its galleries hold sculpture, jewellery, coins and pottery from Chandravalli, Mohenjo-daro and Halebidu, including the Halmidi inscription, the earliest Kannada text. One ticket also covers the Venkatappa Art Gallery next door, 600-plus works by K. Venkatappa.

1.Someshwara Temple
The sanctum, vestibule and pillared hall here date to the Chola period, roughly the 12th-13th century, among the oldest standing structures in the city — centuries older than Bangalore itself. Kempe Gowda I added the maha-mandapa and eastern gopuram in the 16th century, after Someshwara led him to buried treasure funding the work, the story goes. Chola stone sits under Vijayanagara ornament here.

One of the biggest lakes inside Bangalore proper, Ulsoor Lake was built up as a tank on the city's eastern edge and now holds several wooded islands near its centre, ringed by rain trees. 4.HAL Aerospace Museum
India's first aerospace museum opened in 2001 on Hindustan Aeronautics Limited's own Bangalore campus, tracing six decades of the country's aircraft industry. Full-size jets and helicopters — a MiG-21, a Sea King, the HJT-36 Sitara trainer, an Ajeet — sit under open-sided hangars alongside engine cutaways, a mock air-traffic-control tower and flight simulators visitors can pay to try.

1.Nandi Hills
A fortified granite hill rising to 1,478m above the plain north of the city, Nandi Hills has been a retreat since the Ganga dynasty and later a Tipu Sultan stronghold — his summer house still stands near the top, beside Tipu's Drop, a sheer cliff on the western edge. The reason to come this early is the view: cloud rolls over the valley most mornings and burns off at sunrise.

1.Mysore Palace
British architect Henry Irwin rebuilt the Wodeyar dynasty's royal seat after a fire, finishing this Indo-Saracenic palace of domes, turrets and grey granite in the early 20th century. Inside, the Kalyana Mantapa's stained-glass dome sits on cast-iron pillars from Glasgow, and two durbar halls carry carved granite columns. Around 97,000 bulbs outline it every Sunday and public holiday after dark.

The Wodeyar maharajas' family deity, Chamundeshwari, is enshrined atop Chamundi Hill, 13km and roughly 1,000 vertical feet above Mysuru. Local Tastes
Masala Dosa
Bangalore's own version: the crisp rice-and-lentil crepe comes stained red with a chilli-garlic smear before the potato goes in, and arrives with butter still melting on it. Served from breakfast until it runs out.
Vidyarthi Bhavan · Central Tiffin Room · Mavalli Tiffin Rooms
Filter Coffee
Coffee from the Karnataka hills, ground fine and dripped through a two-part steel filter, then poured between tumbler and dabarah until it foams. Ordered by strength of decoction, not cup size, and drunk standing up.
Brahmin's Coffee Bar · Airlines Hotel · Mavalli Tiffin Rooms
Bisi Bele Bath
Rice, lentils, tamarind and a roasted spice blend cooked into one hot dish — the name means hot lentil rice. Karnataka's comfort food, served with a spoon of ghee and fried boondi on top for crunch.
Mavalli Tiffin Rooms · Halli Mane · Vidyarthi Bhavan
Mysore Pak
Gram flour, sugar and a startling quantity of ghee, cooked until it sets into a sweet that crumbles as it is lifted. Invented in the Mysore palace kitchens down the road, and sold by weight all over the city.

Getting Around
Uber
Foreign number: accepted. — An account opened abroad keeps working here, and a fresh sign-up sends its code by SMS, so the home SIM has to stay live for roaming texts.
The predictable way across a city whose street numbering follows no sequence a visitor can read: the fare is fixed before you accept and the route is on screen. Cars thin out and cancel at peak hours; Uber Auto puts the same app on a rickshaw, costs a fraction, and gets down lanes a sedan cannot.
uber.com
Ola
Foreign number: not accepted. — Sign-up requires an Indian mobile number for the OTP, and a foreign handset never clears that verification step.
The home-grown rival, and on Bangalore's streets it often has cars where Uber has none, with an auto-rickshaw tier that is the cheapest metered way across town. The catch for a visitor is the door: without an Indian SIM there is no account, so it is worth knowing about mainly if you are travelling with someone local.
olacabs.com
Rapido
Foreign number: not accepted. — Registration is India-only and the code goes to an Indian number, so a foreign handset cannot open an account at all.
Bangalore's answer to gridlock: a bike taxi that filters between stopped traffic and turns a 40-minute crawl into 15 minutes, plus the city's most reliable auto-rickshaw hailing. Helmets are provided and the ride is not for everyone, but locals use it more than anything else at rush hour.
rapido.bike

Worth Knowing

Bangalore runs on two clocks, and the traffic decides which one you are on

Distance means nothing here and time means everything. The four stops in this guide sit inside a six-kilometre triangle, and the same leg that takes nine minutes at ten in the morning takes forty at six in the evening. Plan the day north to south the way it is written, keep the long hop out to Rajajinagar for early, and treat any crossing of the centre after 5pm as a half-hour whatever the estimate promises.

The city sits at nearly a thousand metres, which is the whole reason it exists as it does. Evenings are cool enough for a layer even in April, the light goes fast and flat after six, and the monsoon arrives twice — once from the southwest in June and again from the northeast in October. Neither is a washout; both mean an umbrella and an hour's patience in the afternoon.

Cash still opens more doors than a foreign card. The gate at Lalbagh, an auto-rickshaw flagged on the street, a filter coffee at a stand-up counter — all of them are simpler with small notes. Almost every counter takes UPI, which visitors cannot use without an Indian bank account, so the tourist's fallback is cash rather than plastic.

Temples ask for bare feet, and shoes go into a rack at the entrance for a small fee or nothing at all. At ISKCON the route is one-way through the complex and doubles back for no one, so leave nothing you need beyond the shoe counter. Shoulders and knees covered is the safe default, and it costs nothing to be the person who was dressed for it.

The Kannada name is Bengaluru — it has been official since 2014 and appears on every station board, timetable and street sign in the city. Bangalore is what most people still say out loud, including locals. Recognising both is worth more here than picking one.
