Read About Bodrum

A harbour town where ancient ambition, maritime craft and Turkish popular culture meet the Aegean.

THE CITY BENEATH THE HOLIDAY

The castle is Bodrum’s easiest landmark to recognise. It divides the waterfront, appears behind the boats and gives a beach photograph an unmistakable setting. Yet the city’s story reaches much further back than those towers. Modern Bodrum occupies ancient Halicarnassus, a Carian city whose name survives in museums, boat operators and local businesses. Keeping both names in mind makes the apparently scattered ruins feel connected.

Halicarnassus belonged to a world in which Greek culture, Anatolian traditions and Persian imperial power overlapped. The historian Herodotus came from the city. Later, Mausolus made it the centre of his rule, moving his capital from Mylasa and transforming the harbour and its surrounding slopes. The surviving theatre and Myndos Gate help suggest the scale of that ancient city beyond today’s waterfront. The history of Halicarnassus supplies the wider context.

A WONDER THAT BECAME A WORD

The Mausoleum asks more of the imagination than the castle. Its foundations cannot reproduce the effect of the great marble tomb that once rose here, but its influence is unusually easy to recognise: Mausolus’s name became the word for a monumental tomb. Built for the ruler and Artemisia II, the monument belonged to the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World.

The site is best understood as the footprint of something much larger. Sculpture and architectural fragments from it are also held at the British Museum, where the Mausoleum gallery presents the monument through its surviving pieces. Seeing the ground plan in Bodrum and studying the reconstructed form are complementary ways of reading a building whose reputation outlived its walls.

STONE WITH MORE THAN ONE HISTORY

The Knights Hospitaller built the Castle of St Peter in the early fifteenth century. Their multinational order left a visible record in the towers and coats of arms. Ottoman additions subsequently changed the complex, and ancient stone from the ruined Mausoleum entered its construction. A single wall can therefore connect different chapters of Bodrum’s past.

Its present role as the Museum of Underwater Archaeology gives that history another direction. The fortress once watched the sea for ships; today, shipwreck finds inside it reveal what ships carried. Courtyards, defensive passages and galleries belong to the same visit, so the architecture deserves attention between exhibits. The Turkish submission on UNESCO’s Tentative List describes these layers. Tentative status is distinct from inscription as a World Heritage Site.

THE VOYAGE AS AN IDEA

Bodrum’s relationship with the sea includes the labour of boatbuilders and sponge divers as well as leisure. Its municipal account of Bodrum’s maritime heritage connects those traditions with the coast’s modern identity. Wooden boats in the harbour belong to that history even when their present purpose is carrying visitors.

The Blue Voyage also has a literary history. Cevat Şakir Kabaağaçlı, known as the Fisherman of Halicarnassus, and fellow writers helped make coastal journeys part of an imaginative rediscovery of this region. Azra Erhat’s writing gave the phrase an enduring place in Turkish culture. A Blue Voyage is thus more than a description of blue water: it carries associations with friendship, antiquity and travelling along the shore.

GoTürkiye’s account of gulets describes traditional wooden sailing vessels that developed into motor-sailing yachts. A short excursion offers a few bays and a return to the same harbour; a longer coastal voyage changes the scale of the experience. Both make the relationship between hills, coves and settlements easier to see from the water.

A DIFFERENT KIND OF PERFORMANCE

Zeki Müren’s former home introduces a more recent cultural chapter. The singer and composer’s costumes, photographs and personal possessions make the visit intimate in scale. They also show how much performance can be expressed through appearance as well as sound. The museum’s official account places those objects within the life of a much-loved Turkish artist.

After the monumental ambitions of the Mausoleum and the military architecture of the castle, a house offers a useful change of perspective. Bodrum becomes a place where someone lived, worked and chose to spend time. That smaller scale is part of the pleasure of the town: an afternoon can move from a major historical site to a domestic room, then return to an ordinary stretch of shore.
