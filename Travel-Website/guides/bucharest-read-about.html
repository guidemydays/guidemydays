Read About Bucharest

A city that survived two centuries of ambition, occupation, and reinvention — and never quite agreed on which version of itself to be.

The city Ceaușescu built over

The single most useful thing to understand about Bucharest is that much of it was demolished within living memory. In 1984, Nicolae Ceaușescu ordered the levelling of roughly a fifth of the historic city — some 9,000 buildings, 40 churches, dozens of synagogues, three monasteries, and entire neighborhoods that had stood since the 18th century — to make room for the Civic Centre he had sketched on a napkin after a state visit to Pyongyang. The centerpiece was to be a boulevard wider than the Champs-Élysées and a palace even larger than the Palace of Versailles.

The Palace of the Parliament rose from this erasure. Construction consumed somewhere between three and six billion dollars (estimates vary wildly, and the regime did not publish its accounts), involved over 700 architects and 20,000 workers, and required the quarrying of nearly half a million tonnes of marble and hundreds of tonnes of crystal and silk carpet. The building has 1,100 rooms, 12 storeys above ground, and eight floors below. Only a small fraction of it has ever been in use. Ceaușescu was executed before he could move in.

The building has 1,100 rooms and eight floors below ground. Ceaușescu never lived to see a single light turned on.

What makes the palace arresting is not its excess — excess is easy to dismiss — but the particular quality of its ambition. Ceaușescu had seen Kim Il-sung's Pyongyang with its triumphal axes and monumental voids, and he wanted that: a capital that communicated the power of the state through sheer spatial dominance. The Boulevard of the Victory of Socialism, now named Bulevardul Unirii, cuts a kilometer-and-a-half straight line through what was once a complex of medieval lanes toward a building that reads as pure authority. It works. The building communicates its intended message clearly. The message is terror.

What came before — Little Paris of the East

Before Ceaușescu, before communism, Bucharest was something else entirely. Travelers who arrived in the late 19th and early 20th centuries used a phrase that has become a cliché precisely because it captured something real: they called it the Little Paris of the East. The phrase was not entirely flattery. Romanian intellectuals and aristocrats of the period were deeply Francophile, sending their children to Paris to study, furnishing their drawing rooms with French furniture, speaking French at dinner when they wanted to demonstrate cultivation. The city's boulevards, its Belle Époque apartment buildings, its department stores and grand hotels, its formal public gardens — all of these were modelled on what Haussmann had done to Paris, filtered through Romanian money and Romanian light.

Walking Calea Victoriei today, the city's oldest commercial artery, you can still read this history in the facades. The National Museum of Art of Romania occupies the former Royal Palace. The CEC Palace — a savings bank, which opened in 1900 — is a confection of glass and stone that would look entirely at home on a Parisian boulevard. The Romanian Athenaeum, completed in 1888 with funds raised by a public subscription campaign bearing the motto "Give a leu for the Athenaeum," is one of the most beautiful concert halls in Europe. These buildings were not imitations. They were the real thing, built by the same architects and craftsmen, from the same pattern books and the same aesthetic convictions.

Communism did not destroy this city all at once. It nationalized the apartment buildings and subdivided the grand rooms into warrens of smaller flats. It let the facades go without maintenance for forty years. It tore down the parts it found inconvenient or ideologically troubling. But the bones survived, and so did the habits. Bucharest restaurants in 2024 still carry a formality of service — a particular emphasis on the table, the wine, the pace of the meal — that feels distinctly different from the functional eating-as-refueling of cities that had different histories.

The Old Town and what the Ottomans left

Curtea Veche — the Old Princely Court — is the oldest surviving structure in Bucharest, and it is a ruin. The foundations and partial walls of the 15th-century palace where Vlad III held court still stand in a walled courtyard off Strada Franceză, surrounded now by the bars and restaurants of the Old Town (Lipscani) district. A statue of Vlad — lean, mustached, impassive — stands at the entrance. The dungeon where he imprisoned his boyar enemies is a damp basement. None of this is glamorous. It is, however, where the city began.

Bucharest's old city grew as a trading post on the route between Constantinople and the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, and the commerce of that position left its marks. The Stavropoleos Monastery, completed in 1724, is the finest example of Brâncovenesc architecture in the city — an ornate synthesis of Orthodox, Ottoman, and Venetian forms developed by the Wallachian prince Constantin Brâncoveanu in the early 18th century. The carved stone of its façade is extraordinary: columns wrapped in vines, semicircular arches filled with geometric lacework, a narthex whose proportions feel borrowed from something much more ancient than any of its specific elements. The scale is intimate — this is not a monument, it is a church — which makes the craftsmanship read all the more intensely.

The Brâncovenesc style synthesizes Orthodox, Ottoman, and Venetian forms — an architecture that could only have been made at this particular crossroads.

The Old Town district around these monuments was Bucharest's merchant quarter for centuries — Lipscani takes its name from Leipzig, whose traders had a street here. The communist era cleared much of it; the post-communist era rebuilt it, somewhat chaotically, as the city's nightlife district. The result is the usual mixture of restoration and reconstruction: some buildings genuine, some facades, the whole thing livelier than it is beautiful. But the monasteries and the ruin at its center are real, and they are worth the effort of reading past the bars.

The Orthodox tradition

Bucharest has a relationship with Orthodox Christianity that is older than its name and deeper than its politics. The Patriarchal Cathedral on Dealul Mitropoliei — the Patriarchal Hill, one of the few literal hills in an otherwise flat city — has been the seat of the Romanian Patriarchate since the 17th century. The climb to it involves a stone staircase and a walk past the Patriarchal Palace, a complex of buildings that manages to feel simultaneously very ancient and very bureaucratic, which is perhaps the nature of all patriarchates. The cathedral itself, built between 1654 and 1668, is painted floor to ceiling inside with images of the saints and the royal history of Wallachia, frescoes renewed over the centuries without ever quite losing their original character.

The Antim Monastery, a short walk downhill, is quieter and less visited. Its founder, Metropolitan Antim Ivireanul — a Georgian by birth who became one of the most important cultural figures of early 18th-century Wallachia — was eventually strangled on Ottoman orders for his political activities and thrown into the Tundzha River. The monastery that bears his name has a carved stone entrance gate whose detail rivals Stavropoleos, and a cloistered garden where the noise of the surrounding streets does not quite reach.

The parks and the north

The character of Bucharest changes north of the old city center. The communist-era apartment blocks give way, gradually, to the great parks of the Floreasca and Herăstrău districts: Cișmigiu, the oldest public garden in the city, laid out by a Viennese landscape architect in the 1840s; and Herăstrău, built around a 74-hectare lake in the 1930s, where on Sunday afternoons the promenade fills with families and cyclists and people rowing boats and eating sunflower seeds from paper cones.

On the western shore of Herăstrău Lake sits the Dimitrie Gusti National Village Museum, which opened in 1936. It is an open-air collection of traditional Romanian architecture — farmhouses, windmills, water mills, wooden churches — relocated from every region of the country and reassembled on 17 hectares of lakeside parkland. The museum is unusual in having been founded not as a nostalgia project but as a serious ethnographic undertaking: Dimitrie Gusti was a sociologist who had spent years documenting rural life, and the museum was built to preserve what industrialization and urbanization were already beginning to erase. Walking through it now, you are walking through a country that no longer exists in quite this form, preserved by people who understood that it would not last.

Across the road from the Village Museum, the Grigore Antipa Natural History Museum houses one of Eastern Europe's more impressive natural history collections, including a suspended blue-whale skeleton that has been hanging in its glass hall since 1906. The museum was rebuilt and re-curated in the 2000s in a way that would not embarrass a natural history museum in any major European city. The whale, which arrived by train from Constanța on the Black Sea coast, is still the best thing in it.
