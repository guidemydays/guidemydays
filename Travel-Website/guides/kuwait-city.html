Local Tastes
Machboos
Kuwait's national dish — fragrant basmati rice slow-cooked with lamb, chicken, or fish in a blend of dried lime, cinnamon, cardamom, and saffron. Found at every traditional Kuwaiti restaurant.
Freej Swaileh · old city area
Jireesh
A Bedouin staple of slow-cooked crushed wheat with chicken broth and spices, thick and warming — most authentic at Freej Swaileh and traditional restaurants in the old city area.
Freej Swaileh · old city area
Murabyan
A coastal classic of aromatic rice cooked with dried shrimp, fried onions, and rose water — a distinctly Kuwaiti flavour rooted in the city's pearling heritage.
Traditional restaurants · old city area
Karak Chai
Kuwait's ubiquitous street tea — strong black tea simmered with cardamom and condensed milk. Sold from roadside stalls and small tea shops for a fraction of a dinar; essential.
Roadside stalls citywide
Luqaimat
Small, crispy deep-fried dough balls drizzled with date syrup or honey, served hot from street vendors and traditional café dessert menus.
Street vendors citywide

Uber operates citywide in Kuwait and is the most reliable ride-hailing option for visitors. Coverage is strong across Kuwait City, Salmiya, and surrounding areas. Kuwait is a car-dependent city — ride-hailing replaces almost all transit; walkable distances between attractions are rare.

Worth Knowing

Dry country, generous hospitality

Kuwait prohibits the sale and consumption of alcohol — this applies to hotels, restaurants, and private residences with no exceptions.

Dress modestly when visiting the Grand Mosque; women are loaned an abaya at the entrance. Cover shoulders and knees at mosques and traditional areas throughout the city.

The best months to visit are October through March; the summer heat makes outdoor sightseeing genuinely unpleasant for most of the day.

Friday is the first day of the weekend — many government museums and some cultural sites close Friday morning and reopen mid-afternoon. Plan day two's itinerary around this.

The Kuwaiti Dinar — KWD — is one of the world's highest-valued currencies; most purchases cost a fraction of a dinar. ATMs accept major international cards widely across the city.

English is widely spoken in hotels, malls, and tourist areas — Arabic phrases are welcomed but not required.

Many restaurants in Kuwait open for lunch from noon and stay open until after midnight, with a mid-afternoon lull. The city comes alive in the evening.
