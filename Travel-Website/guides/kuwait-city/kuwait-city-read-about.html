Read About Kuwait City

A small Gulf emirate that turned desert and oil into one of the world's wealthiest cities — shaped by Bedouin traders, British protection, a devastating invasion, and a remarkable recovery.

ORIGINS ON THE GULF

Kuwait City grew from a modest fishing and pearling settlement established around 1613 near the head of the Arabian Gulf. The name "Kuwait" derives from the Arabic kut, a diminutive of the word for a small fort — a reference to the fortress built by the Bani Khalid tribe to control the bay. By the late seventeenth century, a branch of the Bani Utub confederation, including the Al-Sabah family, had settled here permanently.

The town's position at the northern tip of the Gulf made it a natural transit point between the Arabian Peninsula, Mesopotamia, and the Indian Ocean trade routes. By the eighteenth century, Kuwait City was a prosperous entrepôt, its merchants exporting dates, horses, and dried fish while importing spices, textiles, and timber from India. The pearling trade was central: at its peak in the nineteenth century, Kuwait operated hundreds of pearling vessels, and the harvest financed a merchant class whose influence would shape the city's culture for generations.

THE AL-SABAH DYNASTY AND BRITISH PROTECTION

The Al-Sabah family established their primacy as Kuwait's rulers in the mid-eighteenth century through a negotiated arrangement with the other leading merchant families — an early form of consultative governance that distinguished Kuwait from the purely autocratic models elsewhere in the Gulf. The current ruling family descends directly from that founding pact.

By 1899, faced with Ottoman pressure from the north and uncertainty about which great power would dominate the region, Sheikh Mubarak Al-Sabah (known as Mubarak the Great) signed a secret agreement with Britain. Kuwait became a British protectorate — nominally independent in its internal affairs but shielded by British foreign policy. This arrangement lasted until independence in 1961, when Kuwait became the first of the Gulf states to gain full sovereignty. Britain recognised the new nation within days; Iraq's initial claim to Kuwait was rebuffed by British troops dispatched at Kuwait's request.

OIL AND TRANSFORMATION

Commercial oil was discovered in 1938 at the Burgan field, which would prove to be one of the largest oil reservoirs ever found. Production was interrupted by the Second World War, but by the 1950s, revenues were flowing on a scale no one had anticipated. Sheikh Abdullah Al-Salem Al-Sabah, who ruled from 1950 to 1965, channeled the income into one of the most ambitious social welfare programmes in the world: free education and healthcare, heavily subsidised utilities, and a construction boom that transformed a modest port town into a modern city within two decades.

The skyline that visitors see today — Kuwait Towers on the waterfront, Liberation Tower cutting the sky at 372 metres — is the physical expression of that transformation. Urban planners from the 1950s and 1960s laid out wide boulevards and ring roads for a car-first city when oil seemed inexhaustible. The result is a capital that functions at automobile scale, with its cultural and commercial life spread across districts connected by expressways rather than walkable streets.

THE IRAQI INVASION AND LIBERATION

On 2 August 1990, Iraqi forces under Saddam Hussein crossed the border and overran Kuwait in less than twelve hours. The emir and much of the government escaped to Saudi Arabia; a resistance operated from within the occupied city while Kuwait's allies organised an international coalition response. The Iraqi occupation lasted seven months, during which much of the city was looted, thousands of Kuwaitis were killed or detained, and infrastructure was systematically damaged.

Operation Desert Storm began on 17 January 1991. Coalition air power dismantled Iraqi positions over six weeks; a ground campaign liberated Kuwait City in less than four days, ending on 26 February 1991. As they retreated, Iraqi forces set fire to over 600 oil wells — the resulting smoke clouds were visible from orbit and the fires burned for months. Liberation Tower, visible from almost everywhere in the city, was built in the immediate aftermath as a literal monument to the recovery. The date of liberation, 25 February, is observed as National Day across the country.

KUWAITI IDENTITY AND CULTURE

Kuwait is a constitutional emirate where Islam is the state religion, Arabic is the official language, and English is widely understood in business and tourism. Kuwaiti society maintains a strong distinction between citizens — who number roughly one-third of the population — and the large expatriate workforce that keeps the economy functioning. Indian, Bangladeshi, Egyptian, and Filipino communities each have significant presences, and the cultural mix is visible in the city's restaurant scene and its working-class neighbourhoods.

The merchant tradition has never entirely disappeared. Kuwaiti families with roots in the pearling and trading era retain social prestige, and the country has a more active civic and parliamentary culture than most of its Gulf neighbours. The National Assembly, established in 1962, is the oldest directly elected legislature in the Gulf region. Debates over the rights of stateless residents (the Bidoon), women's political participation, and government accountability have been part of Kuwaiti public life for decades.

THE CITY TODAY

Kuwait City holds roughly 150,000 people, but the greater metropolitan area stretches south and east through Salmiya, Hawalli, and Farwaniya, encompassing more than three million residents. The city centre along Arabian Gulf Street — from the National Museum past Kuwait Towers to the Sharq Marina — is the formal face Kuwait presents to the world: broad waterfront boulevards, embassies, the green domes of the Grand Mosque, and the glass towers of the financial district.

Away from the Gulf, the character changes. Souq Mubarakiyah, dating to the nineteenth century, remains a functional covered market selling spices, coffee, dates, perfume, and metalwork alongside mobile phone cases and football shirts. It is the oldest intact piece of the pre-oil city. The Avenues Mall, by contrast, is among the largest shopping centres in the Middle East — an indoor city in its own right, with themed districts, canals, and a food hall that tracks global consumption trends in real time. Both are Kuwait City at full authenticity, at opposite ends of the economic century that separates them.
