Food Delivery
Rappi
Foreign number: not accepted. — Rappi's sign-up only issues verification codes to local numbers from its live countries, so a foreign SIM never receives the code.
Colombia's dominant delivery app by far, covering nearly every restaurant in El Poblado and beyond; also handles groceries and pharmacy runs, but new accounts need a Colombian number to register.
rappi.com.co
DiDi Food
Foreign number: accepted — the sign-up form takes a foreign country code and delivers the SMS code over international roaming, though a local SIM is more reliable.
A smaller second option to Rappi with a lighter restaurant list, but useful as a backup when Rappi has no delivery slots at peak hours.
didiglobal.com

Getting Around
Uber
Foreign number: accepted — international numbers work at sign-up and international cards are accepted for payment.
Operates in Medellín in a legal gray area — drivers sometimes ask riders to sit in front to avoid attention — but is reliable, cashless and the most familiar option for visitors.
uber.com
Cabify
Foreign number: accepted — sign-up takes an international number and Cabify accepts foreign-issued cards.
A fully legal, licensed alternative to Uber with a similar fleet and price point; often has shorter wait times late at night in El Poblado.
cabify.com
inDrive
Foreign number: accepted — sign-up takes an international number, though fares are settled mostly in cash so a local card is not required either.
A name-your-price app popular with locals for being cheaper than Uber or Cabify; fares are negotiated with the driver before the ride starts.
indrive.com

Packing for a Hike
Worth Knowing

A city that rebuilt itself in one generation, and it shows on every hill

Every cable car in this guide — to Comuna 13's overlooks, to Parque Arví — runs on the same fare card as the Metro below it. Medellín built the system specifically to connect hillside barrios once considered no-go zones into the transit network on equal footing with downtown, and locals treat the ride as ordinary transit, not a tourist gondola. Riding it end to end is one of the cheapest, clearest ways to see how the city's geography and its social history are the same story.

Fruit here is not a novelty stop — it is a real category of local eating. Vendors around Plaza Botero and the Provenza cafés both keep Colombian fruit rarely sold outside the country: lulo, granadilla, mamoncillo, zapote. It is worth trying a few plain before assuming they only work as juice.

Medellín sits at 1,495 meters, which gives it the "eternal spring" climate the tourism board advertises — daytime highs stay in the low-to-mid 20s°C year-round. The trade-off is the afternoon: rain arrives fast and often, especially April to May and October to November, so an afternoon outdoor stop such as Guatapé, the hikes or Parque Arví is safest scheduled for the morning.

Taxis and rideshares in El Poblado routinely ask riders to sit in the front seat rather than the back — a legacy of a period when a passenger in the back of an unmarked car read as a kidnapping risk to police checkpoints. It is a harmless local habit now, not a safety signal either way.
