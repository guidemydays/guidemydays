1. Plaza Grande
Quito’s central square since the 16th century, ringed by the Carondelet Palace — seat of government — the Metropolitan Cathedral, the Archbishop’s Palace and the Municipal Palace. A French-cast Independence Monument at the center marks the 1809 uprising against Spanish rule — one of the first in South America.

2. Catedral Metropolitana de Quito
South America’s oldest cathedral foundation, begun in 1535 and completed in 1799, blending Baroque, Gothic-Mudéjar and Neoclassical styles on the southwest side of Plaza Grande. A narrow colonial staircase climbs to a rooftop walkway with views over the historic center; Mariscal Sucre, hero of Ecuadorian independence, is entombed inside.

3. Iglesia de La Compañía de Jesús
Quito’s Baroque masterpiece, built 1605–1765 by Jesuit architects on a façade of carved volcanic stone. Inside, roughly seven tonnes of gold leaf cover the nave, side altars and central dome — the single most photographed interior in the city’s Historic Center.

1. Basílica del Voto Nacional
The largest neo-Gothic basilica in the Americas, begun in 1892 and never formally finished — a running joke holds that completing it would trigger the biblical end of the world. Ecuadorian touches replace European gargoyles with carved iguanas, tortoises and armadillos. Steep ladders and a narrow catwalk lead up through the clock tower to the base of the spires.

2. Casa del Alabado Museum
Pre-Columbian art displayed as art rather than artefact, inside a restored 17th-century colonial house near San Francisco. More than 500 pieces from Ecuador’s ancient cultures — ceramics, gold and stone figures spanning 5,000 years — are arranged thematically around candlelit courtyards.

4. El Panecillo
A 200-metre volcanic hill dividing north and south Quito, capped since 1975 by a 41-metre aluminium Virgin of Quito — the world’s only major Marian statue with wings, modelled on Bernardo de Legarda’s 1734 “dancing Virgin.” A narrow internal staircase climbs through the hollow statue to a platform at her feet, with the whole Historic Center laid out below.

1. TelefériQo
One of the world’s highest cable cars, climbing 1,200 vertical metres up the east flank of Pichincha volcano in 18 minutes. The upper station at Cruz Loma sits at 3,945m, above the cloud line more often than not, with the whole of Quito’s 2,850m valley spread out below and a marked trail continuing toward Rucu Pichincha’s summit for those who want to keep climbing.

2. Capilla del Hombre
Oswaldo Guayasamín’s final work, a purpose-built chapel-museum overlooking the city, completed in 2002 after the painter’s death. Murals, sculpture and an eternal flame form a memorial to the suffering and resilience of Latin America’s peoples; the adjoining house museum holds his personal collection of pre-Columbian and colonial art.

Quito’s central park, 67 hectares of lawns, a boating lake and running paths ringed by the city’s financial district. Day 4
From Hotel: 30 min → Ciudad Mitad del Mundo
1. Ciudad Mitad del Mundo
A 30-metre monument built in 1979–1982 to mark the equator, following the 18th-century Franco-Spanish Geodesic Mission that first fixed its location. A yellow line across the plaza draws every visitor for the straddle-the-hemispheres photo, even though GPS puts the true equator 240m to the north. The nine floors hold an ethnographic museum of Ecuador’s indigenous peoples.

2. Museo Intiñan
A privately run science-and-culture museum 200m from the official monument, built along a line its owners measured with GPS as the true equator. Guides run hands-on demonstrations along the line — balancing an egg on a nail, water draining from a basin — alongside exhibits on Amazonian and Andean indigenous cultures, including shrunken-head funerary tradition.

3. Reserva Geobotánica Pululahua
One of only two volcanic craters in the world inhabited and farmed, a few kilometres from the equator monument. From the Ventanillas viewpoint, a 5km-wide caldera opens below — patchwork fields and a scatter of farmhouses on the crater floor, usually wreathed in cloud by early afternoon. The reserve is Ecuador’s only geobotanical protected area.

1. Cotopaxi National Park
Ecuador’s second-oldest national park, protecting the páramo grassland around Cotopaxi, one of the world’s highest continuously active volcanoes at 5,897m. The flat trail around Laguna de Limpiopungo — a shallow lake at 3,800m ringed by wild horses and the odd Andean gull — gives the park’s clearest reflected view of the snow-capped cone on a still morning.

Local Tastes
Locro de Papa
A thick potato-and-cheese soup topped with avocado and a strip of fried pork skin — Andean comfort food and one of the dishes Quiteños miss most when abroad.
Achiote · Vista Hermosa
Hornado
Whole roast pig marinated in chicha and Andean spices, slow-cooked until the skin cracks, served with mote, llapingachos and avocado. A market and Sunday-lunch staple across the highlands.
Café Plaza Grande Restaurante
Llapingachos
Pan-fried mashed-potato patties stuffed with cheese, served with peanut sauce, fried egg and chorizo. Ecuador's answer to the potato cake, found on nearly every traditional menu.
Miskay · La Bodega de Cantúña
Canelazo
A hot cocktail of aguardiente — or naranjilla juice for the alcohol-free version — cinnamon and sugar cane, sipped against Quito’s cool Andean evenings, the city’s answer to mulled wine.

Empanadas de Morocho
Griddled corn-dough empanadas made from ground white morocho corn instead of wheat flour, filled with cheese — an Old Town street-food staple sold from carts around the historic churches.

Worth Knowing

The best-preserved colonial center in the Americas sits on the equator, 2,850 meters up

Quito was the first city anywhere to be named a UNESCO World Heritage Site, in 1978, alongside the Galápagos — and the reason is scale, not a single monument. The Historic Center holds roughly 130 blocks of intact 16th-to-18th-century architecture, more than any other capital in Latin America, because Quito was never a major target for the redevelopment booms that erased colonial cores elsewhere. Walking between Plaza Grande, La Compañía and San Francisco covers ground that would take three or four separate old-town visits in most other South American capitals.

The altitude is the one practical fact worth planning around. At 2,850 meters, Quito sits higher than most travelers have ever slept, and the effects — breathlessness on stairs, a fast pulse at rest, an early headache — usually show up the first afternoon, not the first hour. They fade within two or three days as the body adjusts. Alcohol and heavy meals make the first night rougher than the altitude alone would; canelazo tastes like mulled wine but carries a real dose of aguardiente on top of a thinner atmosphere already doing its own work.

The equator itself is a small, honest joke on the whole city. The 1736 French Geodesic Mission fixed its location using the best astronomy of the age, and the resulting monument at Mitad del Mundo is a genuine engineering landmark — just measured about 240 meters south of where GPS now puts the true line. The Intiñan Museum next door built its own line using modern equipment, which is why two equator monuments stand a few minutes' walk apart, both claiming the same latitude with a straight face.
