Read About Tulum

A walled Mayan port that spent a thousand years facing the sea is now facing something harder to navigate: the world's attention.

The cliff and the coast

Tulum sits at the southeastern edge of the Yucatan Peninsula, where the jungle meets a thin strip of Caribbean beach and the land ends in a low limestone cliff. The ruins of the ancient Mayan city occupy that cliff — one of the few Mayan sites in Mexico built with the sea in view, and the only major one that was also a functioning walled city at the time of Spanish contact in the early sixteenth century. The walls, some three to five meters high and extending on three sides, protected a settlement that traded jade, obsidian, copper bells, and turquoise across the Caribbean for several centuries. The sea was not a backdrop but a highway.

Most visitors spend an hour at the ruins and then go to the beach below. This is not a mistake — the beach is exceptional, framed by the cliff and reached by a narrow path where iguanas sit motionless on warm rocks. But it means missing the particularity of what Tulum is. Unlike Chichén Itzá or Cobá, which sit deep in the jungle on ancient trade routes, Tulum was a maritime city. The Temple of the Descending God — named for the unusual upside-down figure carved above doorways throughout the site — may represent the diving bee deity, the honey bee being one of the Yucatan's most valued trade commodities. The city faced east across open water toward the horizon, which is the same direction the morning light comes from and the same direction everything in Tulum has faced since.

The cenote system

The Yucatan Peninsula is a slab of limestone so porous that it has almost no surface rivers. Rain falls, percolates through the rock, and moves underground in a vast connected aquifer. Where the ceiling of that underground network collapses, a cenote opens — the word comes from the Mayan ts'onot, meaning "hole with water." The peninsula has thousands of them, distributed in a rough ring that traces the edge of the Chicxulub crater, the impact site of the asteroid that ended the Cretaceous period 66 million years ago. The impact fractured the limestone in a pattern that still shapes where water moves today.

For the Maya, cenotes were not simply water sources. They were openings into Xibalba, the underworld — places where the natural world broke open and the sacred began. Offerings recovered from cenotes across the Yucatan include jade, pottery, incense, and human remains. Gran Cenote, a few kilometers west of the ruins, is one of the most visited in the region: a collapsed cavern with a clear shallow pool, connected to deeper passages that divers have mapped for kilometers in every direction. The water is cold and strikingly transparent, stained a faint turquoise by the minerals dissolved in it. Stalactites formed above the current waterline during past ice ages, when sea levels were lower and the cavern was dry — the cenotes carry the memory of a different climate in their ceilings.

The Maya had understood for centuries what geologists confirmed in the 1990s: the cenote ring is the footprint of the impact that ended the dinosaurs.

The underground system connects Gran Cenote to others further inland, and to the sea. In some passages, freshwater and saltwater meet in a halocline — a visible boundary where the two densities stack without mixing, creating a ghostly shimmer that divers describe as looking through two different windows at once. The aquifer is the region's only freshwater source and one of the largest and most ecologically complex underground river systems on earth.

Beyond the ruins: Cobá and the interior

Forty-five kilometers northwest of Tulum, on the edge of two small lakes in the jungle interior, the ruins of Cobá occupy a different register entirely. Where Tulum is compact and coastal, Cobá is massive and overgrown — a city that at its peak in the Classic period (roughly 600–900 CE) may have had a population of 50,000, connected to the rest of the Mayan world by a network of raised stone causeways called sacbés, several of which are still partly intact and walkable. The main pyramid, Nohoch Mul, rises 42 meters and was until recently climbable; the steps are steep enough that a rope down the center was necessary on the descent, and the view from the top — a flat green canopy broken by no visible horizon — gave a sense of the scale of the forest the Maya inhabited rather than cleared.

Cobá's size, and the density of the jungle around it, mean that most of the site remains unexcavated. Mounds that look like natural hills are almost certainly pyramids or residential platforms; estimates of the total number of structures run into the thousands. Walking the sacbé between the main group and the Macanxoc group in the late afternoon, when the light comes low through the trees and the sound is almost exclusively birds, it is possible to feel something that the major restored sites don't always permit: a sense of the city as it was, embedded in landscape rather than displayed against it.

Sian Ka'an and what the coast was

South of Tulum's hotel zone, the character of the coast changes abruptly. The Sian Ka'an Biosphere Reserve — whose name in Mayan means "where the sky is born" — covers 528,000 hectares of tropical forest, wetlands, mangroves, and barrier reef along the Quintana Roo coast. It was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987, one of the first such designations in Mexico, and protects one of the most complete coastal ecosystems in the Caribbean basin. The channels cut through the wetlands by the ancient Maya are still navigable; floating through them on guided excursions, propelled by current with no mechanical noise, is one of the ways the landscape around Tulum remains accessible without being consumed by it.

The reef that runs parallel to this coast is part of the Mesoamerican Barrier Reef, the second-largest coral reef system in the world after Australia's Great Barrier Reef. The snorkeling at Akumal Bay, 30 kilometers north of Tulum, is organized around the green sea turtles that feed on the seagrass beds there; boats are prohibited close to shore, and snorkelers share the shallow water with turtles that have been documented using the bay for decades. The population is monitored by a local conservation organization, and the density of turtles in this one bay — some mornings you count more than a dozen in a single hour — reflects both the health of the seagrass and the limitations imposed on the surrounding development.

Valladolid and the colonial interior

One hundred and sixty kilometers northwest of Tulum, on the far side of the Yucatan Peninsula's relatively flat interior, Valladolid is a colonial city that functions as a working center for the surrounding agricultural region rather than a tourist destination that has learned to tolerate visitors. The streets are on a Spanish grid, the houses face inward to private courtyards, and the market operates on its own schedule regardless of what the restaurants around the main square are doing. The Convento de San Bernardino de Siena, built in the sixteenth century partly over a Mayan cenote, is one of the oldest colonial churches in the Yucatan; the cenote that runs beneath part of the convent grounds is accessible from inside the complex.

Valladolid sits in Yucatán state rather than Quintana Roo, which means crossing a time zone boundary on the drive — Quintana Roo stays on Eastern Standard Time year-round, while Yucatán observes Central Time and shifts with daylight saving. The transition is unmarked on the road and easy to miss; the consequence is an hour added to the apparent duration of every journey in that direction, which is why a Valladolid day trip from Tulum typically requires an early start.

The transformation of the town

For most of the twentieth century, the modern town of Tulum — the "Pueblo" — was a service settlement for the ejido farming communities of the region, connected to the outside world primarily by the main road from Cancún. The ruins drew visitors beginning in the 1970s and 1980s, but the tourism of that era was small-scale: backpackers on the Ruta Maya circuit, a handful of cabañas on the beach, a famously difficult road south to the ruins. The international airport is in Cancún, two hours north, and the development of Cancún's resort corridor in the 1970s and 1980s transformed the economics of the whole peninsula.

What happened to Tulum in the 2010s was more specific. The town was discovered by a design-oriented, social-media-adjacent tourism that valorized its existing qualities — the bohemian beach energy, the jungle setting, the relative lack of the large hotel infrastructure that dominates Cancún and Playa del Carmen — and then proceeded to commercialize them. The thatched palapa restaurants and hammock hotels that made the beach attractive in 2010 were progressively replaced by architecture that aspired to something between an outdoor boutique and a wellness retreat. By the mid-2010s, Tulum had become a shorthand in the international travel press for a particular aesthetic: linen clothing, raw-edge wood furniture, fresh coconut drinks, cenote swims photographed from above.

The paradox of Tulum is that the qualities tourists came to find were partly destroyed by the volume of tourists who found them.

The pressure this created on the underlying landscape was real. The cenote system, which the tourist industry depended on for both swimming and the visual identity it was selling, was also the only source of fresh water for the region's growing population. A long-running conflict over sewage treatment — inadequate for decades, contaminating the aquifer — became, by the 2020s, one of the defining infrastructure crises of the Riviera Maya. The Tulum that exists now is somewhere between a destination that has been thoroughly found and one that has not yet fully worked out what to do with having been found. The ruins are still on the cliff. The sea is still east.
