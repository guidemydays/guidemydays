Local Tastes
Couscous
Tunisia's staple grain dish, steamed and served with lamb, fish, or vegetables in a spiced red harissa broth — richer and spicier than its Moroccan cousin.
Dar El Jeld · Fondouk El Attarine
Brik à l'Œuf
A paper-thin malsouka pastry folded around a whole egg, parsley, and tuna or minced meat, then deep-fried until crackling — eaten hot, in one bite, before the yolk breaks.
Fondouk El Attarine
Tunisian Mint Tea with Pine Nuts
Strong green tea brewed with fresh mint and sugar, topped with a scattering of toasted pine nuts — the everyday drink of Medina cafés, sipped slowly over conversation.

Worth Knowing

A few things that make a Tunis trip go smoother

Tunis reads as two cities stacked on top of each other: the Medina's tenth-century lanes end abruptly at Bab El Bhar, and beyond it Avenue Habib Bourguiba's French colonial arcades and Art Nouveau facades could pass for a boulevard in Nice. The gate itself — also called Porte de France — is the hinge between the two, and standing in it you can look one way into souks and the other into café terraces.

Carthage, Sidi Bou Said and La Marsa all sit on the same 19-km TGM commuter line, so a reader with more time than this itinerary allows can string them into one long day by rail instead of by car — the line runs from before dawn past midnight and a ticket costs a fraction of a taxi.

The call to prayer from the Zitouna Mosque's minaret is audible across the whole Medina five times a day; the loudest and most atmospheric is the Maghrib call at sunset, timed to land while the souks are still open but the light has gone golden.
